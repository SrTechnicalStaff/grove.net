Harbor archive — first pass
Field notes · 2 placements

The harbor office kept two sets of books. One recorded arrivals, the other recorded repairs, and after 1931 the two stop agreeing with each other.

The disagreement is the useful part. It dates the year the second pier was rebuilt, which none of the surviving photographs establish on their own.

Everything before that year reads as one continuous account. Everything after has to be assembled from the repair book, the county ledger, and whatever the pilot station chose to write down that week.

The second pier appears in the repair book eleven times before it appears in the arrivals book once. Whatever it was used for in those years, it was not receiving traffic.

Two hands wrote the arrivals book. The first is careful and abbreviates nothing. The second writes fast and drops the vessel class entirely after 1936.

There is a third hand in the margins of both books, later, in pencil. It corrects dates and nothing else, and it is almost certainly an archivist rather than a clerk.

Field notes · 1 placement

Four months of 1938 are missing from both books, and the gap sits exactly where the storm damage should be.

A lost volume is the likeliest explanation. The archivist thinks the binder was never returned after the county borrowed it, and that it may still be in a box in the annex.

Weather is recorded only when it stopped work. A quiet year in these books is not a quiet year on the water.

Tide tables, 1912–1948
Archive · 1 placement

Tide tables were printed locally until 1948, when the county took the work over.

The local printings carry marginal notes in at least three hands, two of them dated. The third appears only beside the spring tides and is probably the harbormaster's own.

This is the only run in the set that is complete from start to finish, and that completeness is what makes the gaps elsewhere legible.

Photographs are dated by the pier, not by the caption. Captions were added in the 1970s by someone working from memory.

They are wrong often enough that they cannot stand as evidence on their own, though they are rarely wrong by more than a few years.

Letters from the pilot station
Archive · 3 placements

The pilot station letters are the only place where the crews are named. Sixty-one names across nine years, most of them appearing once.

Where a name repeats it usually belongs to a family rather than to a person. Three generations of Ellises appear between 1912 and 1940 without a single first name written the same way twice.

The letters use a house style that changes twice. Salutations are formal until 1921, plain until 1934, and formal again after the station changed hands.

That change is the clearest dating tool in the whole set, and it is worth checking against the repair book before trusting it.

Archive · 1 placement

It records a payment for timber in the quarter the letters describe a fire, larger than any other timber entry in the decade.

That is not proof. It is the kind of agreement between two indifferent sources that is worth writing down and returning to.

The harbor office kept two sets of books. One recorded arrivals, the other recorded repairs, and after 1931 the two stop agreeing with each other.

The disagreement is the useful part. It dates the year the second pier was rebuilt, which none of the surviving photographs establish on their own.

Everything before that year reads as one continuous account. Everything after has to be assembled from the repair book, the county ledger, and whatever the pilot station chose to write down that week.

The books

The second pier appears in the repair book eleven times before it appears in the arrivals book once. Whatever it was used for in those years, it was not receiving traffic.

Two hands wrote the arrivals book. The first is careful and abbreviates nothing. The second writes fast and drops the vessel class entirely after 1936.

There is a third hand in the margins of both books, later, in pencil. It corrects dates and nothing else, and it is almost certainly an archivist rather than a clerk.

The arrivals book uses a house shorthand for cargo that nobody has written down anywhere. Six of the abbreviations are obvious, four are not, and two appear only once.

Where the two books can be matched, they agree on dates to within a day. The disagreements are all about what happened, never about when.

Four months of 1938 are missing from both books, and the gap sits exactly where the storm damage should be.

A lost volume is the likeliest explanation. The archivist thinks the binder was never returned after the county borrowed it, and that it may still be in a box in the annex.

The annex

The annex is not catalogued. She offered to open it, with the caution that nothing in it has been listed since the move.

Boxes there are labelled by department rather than by year, which means the repair material is likely split across at least four of them.

The annex smells of damp, which is worth recording. If the boxes have been wet the paper will not survive handling and the visit becomes a photography job.

Assume two visits. One to find the boxes, one to read them, and no photography until the second.

Weather is recorded only when it stopped work. A quiet year in these books is not a quiet year on the water.

The winter of 1934 has one line for January and nothing else until March, which either means an easy season or a clerk who had given up.

The tables

Tide tables were printed locally until 1948, when the county took the work over.

The local printings carry marginal notes in at least three hands, two of them dated. The third appears only beside the spring tides and is probably the harbormaster's own.

This is the only run in the set that is complete from start to finish, and that completeness is what makes the gaps elsewhere legible.

The 1921 volume is the best preserved and the least useful. It covers a year in which almost nothing happened, and it survives because nobody ever opened it.

Three of the tables have prices printed on the cover. They rise by a third across the run, which is the only inflation figure available anywhere in the set.

The tables also carry the only drawing in the whole set: a sketch of the mooring at the foot of the 1921 volume, unsigned.

It is drawn to no scale and is probably a reminder rather than a record, but it shows a bollard that appears in no photograph.

The letters

The pilot station letters are the only place where the crews are named. Sixty-one names across nine years, most of them appearing once.

Where a name repeats it usually belongs to a family rather than to a person. Three generations of Ellises appear between 1912 and 1940 without a single first name written the same way twice.

The ledger

The county ledger is the only source in the set that gives money. It is also the only one that survives complete, which is a warning about what survives and why.

It records a payment for timber in the quarter the letters describe a fire, larger than any other timber entry in the decade.

That is not proof. It is the kind of agreement between two indifferent sources that is worth writing down and returning to.

The ledger's hand does not change once in forty years, which is either one very long career or a fair copy made later.

If it is a fair copy, everything it agrees with becomes weaker rather than stronger, and that is worth settling before anything is built on it.

The ledger's entries for the harbour stop for eighteen months in 1943 and resume without comment. The war is never mentioned in any source here.

Sums are recorded to the penny throughout, including a payment of one shilling for a lost key, which suggests the ledger was audited rather than kept for use.

The repair book

The repair book is the only volume that was ever indexed, and the index is wrong after 1940 in a way that looks deliberate.

Entries are grouped by trade rather than by date, so a single job can appear four times without ever being described in full.

The index was written in a different ink from the entries and probably added later, which would explain why it stops being reliable exactly where the entries get inconvenient.

Carpentry is always listed first, which flatters the carpenters and makes any job involving stone almost impossible to date.

Stone work is described by the yard, timber by the foot, and labour by the day. Converting any of it into a comparison takes longer than reading it.

One entry in 1939 reads only “as agreed”. It is the single most frustrating line in the whole set.

The pier drawings are held elsewhere and were not consulted. Their existence is noted here twice, both times as a reference number.

Those numbers are worth carrying to the county office. They are the only thread out of this set into another one.

What is missing

What is missing is consistent: anything that would have shown fault. No source here explains the 1938 gap, and the silence is the same silence in every hand.

Nothing in the set is a copy of anything else. Every hand is writing from its own position, and the disagreements are the evidence rather than the noise.

Every source here was kept for a purpose that was not history. That is the reason they disagree, and the reason they can be trusted about the small things.

TEXT LED · NOTES ON 1010 / 16
01The measure is the constantBoth pages set the same line at the same size. The line a person reads is the one thing a narrower window is never allowed to change.
02Columns are what give wayThree measures become two. The column count is a function of how much room there is, and it is the first thing to move when there is less.
03Then the page count risesTwo pages become four. Less room per page means more pages — the reading gets longer, never smaller.
04Still landscape, still the same formThe narrow page is a smaller wide page, not a tall one. A narrow window never swaps the reading for a portrait book block; that would change the grammar instead of reflowing it.
05The order in which room is given upColumns first, then pages, and nothing after that. Type size, line length, and completeness are not on the list.
06Where a person was, they stayReflowing to more pages keeps the piece they were reading in view. The page number under it changes; their place does not.
